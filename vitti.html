monica vitti in sheffield

August 2026

What do Italian screen legend Monica Vitti, Tony Blair's father-in-law, and the Tinsley Cooling Towers have in common? For a few days in the late summer of 1967, they all found themselves starring in a revenge thriller being filmed in Sheffield. The towers spent a bit longer there, admittedly.

Mario Monicelli's 1968 comedy thriller La ragazza con la pistola follows Assunta (Vitti), a Sicilian woman who, abandoned by Vincenzo (Carlo Giuffrè), the man who “dishonoured” her, pursues him across Britain with a pistol in her bag to restore her family's name. From Edinburgh to Sheffield, and on to Bath and London, the journey of revenge gradually becomes one of unexpected liberation.

Wildly popular in Italy, the film was nominated for the Academy Award for Best Foreign Language Film and Vitti won the David di Donatello, Italy's top film award, for Best Actress, emerging as one of the continent's leading comic stars. Despite spending much of its runtime here, the film was never shown in UK cinemas.

The film's British locations trace a route from north to south. After running out of luck in Edinburgh, Assunta continues her journey to the city of Sheffield. A bus ride takes her there, the English landscape rolling past, a sign pointing the way to The Strines Inn. Upon arrival and a walk down Granville Street, she encounters a young man, John (Anthony Booth), whose shoes she notes as Italian, enlisting him in her quest for vengeance.

After a hair-raising ride in a fancy sports car and an argument outside Steel, Peech and Tozer's Templeborough steelworks, John takes her to a disco at Hyde Park Flats to search for Vincenzo. Here she is introduced to the swinging world by way of the fashions of the young people, along with their music and style of dancing. She also wallops Johnny Briggs (later known as Weatherfield knicker magnate Mike Baldwin) over the head with her handbag.

I was left with a question. When exactly were they in Sheffield?

Logging on to the archives of various publications with my not-so-great Italian, I started searching. It paid off. Buried in a dispatch filed from the set after the crew had moved south, there was a remark from Vitti herself. “I liked Sheffield,” she said, “as ugly as it may be, it has a certain personality: the factories, the mountains of rubble, the darkness.”

The interview appeared in the Stampa Sera of 27–28 September, and by then the production had already left Sheffield behind for Bath, London, and Brighton. That gave me a far edge: the shoot was over by late September. But I could tighten it. An earlier dispatch in the same paper, dated 13–14 September, confirmed the unit had already wrapped in Sheffield and moved to Bath. So they were done and dusted in South Yorkshire by mid-month, not merely by the 27th. Hence the scenes, usually described as cold and wintry, were filmed earlier than I'd assumed. What looks like January on screen was Sheffield at the tail end of summer.

The near edge came into focus in l'Unità, on 12 August: a short piece on Vitti leaving Roma Termini to take the train the whole way to Edinburgh. “I'm terrified of flying,” she confessed to some journalists before leaving, “so I prefer to travel by train, even if this time it will take me two days to get there.” Two days from Rome put her in Scotland on the 14th of August. Edinburgh first, then the move down to Sheffield.

That left a bracket: mid-August at one end, mid-September at the other, Sheffield somewhere inside it. The clincher came in the South Yorkshire Times of 2 September. Alongside confirmation that the film crew had already arrived in town was an appeal for local extras “laden with flower power”. The Italian press had drawn the outline from a thousand miles off; the local paper, reporting from its own doorstep and presumably with no knowledge of either dispatch, slotted the date neatly into the middle of it.

For what it's worth, this glamorous piece, in which “Monicelli trained his cameras on beautiful people, dancing merrily at Sheffield's Hyde Park Flats” appears between a notice for “Britain's premier sheep dog trials” at Longshaw Pastures, near Grindleford, and an advertisement for a “£5 darts handicap” event at the Station Hotel in Conisbrough. One has to assume that the Italian press were equally unaware of these.

the city he found

The film's comedy runs on a clash of cultures: Sicilian honour codes versus swinging Britain. But the Britain Assunta arrives in was itself in the middle of a legal revolution. The Sexual Offences Act 1967 had received Royal Assent on 27 July; the Abortion Act followed in October. Monicelli's camera, chasing a Sicilian woman's pursuit of “honour”, was inadvertently filming a country that was dismantling its own old moral certainties while Italy's Fascist-era matrimonio riparatore, the very law the film satirises, remained on the books for another fourteen years.

The steelworks outside which Assunta and John have their first fight were themselves caught in a different kind of transformation, having been nationalised on 28 July. As recently as 1965, the last of six state-of-the-art Electric Arc Furnaces had been switched on at Templeborough's Electric Melting Shop, making it the world's largest electric steel plant. The factory ceased operations in 1993 but survives today as the Magna Science Adventure Centre.

Then onto Hyde Park Flats. The opening ceremony was performed on 23 June 1966 by Queen Elizabeth The Queen Mother, giving a speech and unveiling a commemorative plaque. Earlier in the day she had also opened the Arts Tower at the University of Sheffield. Followed by “music, mirth, and magic” in the tenants' meeting hall, “a grand concert” on the precinct, and “a young people's dance” in the Hyde Park Stadium social club room, optimism abounded.

Hailed as a streets-in-the-sky utopia, they would be described just twenty years later by one resident as “the worst place in the world”.

The “white heat” speech was 1963. Wilson's government came to power in 1964 with genuine transformative ambition. By 1967, when Monicelli's cameras were rolling, that optimism was already fraying. Just months after the Italians departed came the devaluation of the pound. The retreat on union reform was still two years away, but the tensions were visible.

These were emblematic of how the Labour government of the time failed to deliver a new Britain forged in the “white heat of scientific revolution”. Harold Wilson's inability to successfully reform union power, most notably retreating on his 1969 In Place of Strife white paper and dealing with rampant strikes, created a public appetite for stronger labour laws. This paved the way for the Thatcher government's aggressive union curbs.

Within little more than a decade, the assumptions underpinning all these projects were beginning to collapse. Industrial confidence gave way to recession; modernist housing became politically unfashionable; Wilson's consensual Britain fractured into industrial conflict. The city Monicelli filmed as an embodiment of modern Britain would soon become one of the symbols of its industrial decline.

what the camera kept

Monicelli wasn't making a documentary about Sheffield, nor was he particularly interested in it. He was looking for somewhere that felt industrial, alien and quintessentially British for Assunta's journey. Yet because he shot on location, and because Italian cinema of the period loved long takes and real environments rather than studio recreation, he inadvertently preserved an extraordinary amount of Britain in 1967.

Take the Hyde Park Flats sequence. In 1967, Monicelli is simply filming fashionable young people dancing in a modern housing development. Today, we see a flagship council estate, barely opened, full of optimism, populated by the generation supposedly benefiting from Wilson's white heat. The meaning of the same footage has completely changed.

Templeborough tells the same story. To Monicelli, it's just a giant British steelworks. To us, it's one of the last moments before British Steel, deindustrialisation and the eventual world of The Full Monty, another Sheffield film that found Oscar recognition, this time dramatising the aftermath of the collapse. Monicelli and Vitti filmed the industry alive and world leading; Cattaneo and Carlyle filmed its corpse. Same city, same gallows humour, almost thirty years apart.

The Italians made their film and took it home. It filled cinemas, won Vitti her award, and went to the Oscars. In the country where so much of it had been shot, no cinema ever showed it. A record of Sheffield at the top of an arc nobody could see was made by a man with no real interest in the city, for audiences a thousand miles away. It would take more than fifty years for La ragazza con la pistola to turn up on British television — Sheffield had been ready for its close-up a long time ago.
